### [1] [On the Origin of Mass Ejection in Failed Supernovae](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05289)
*Daniel A. Paradiso,Sarah Vallejo,Eric R. Coughlin*

Main category: astro-ph.HE

TL;DR: This paper analyzes the stability of self-similar solutions for weak shockwaves in failed supernovae, finding that larger Mach number solutions are unstable while smaller ones are stable, and that above a critical mass loss threshold the shock strengthens toward the strong limit, with implications for progenitor-dependent observables.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: To understand the stability and evolution of weak shockwaves generated by neutrinos during failed supernovae (implosions forming black holes), and to determine what physical parameters dictate whether the shock strengthens or remains self-similar, thereby explaining differences in transient luminosity across progenitor types.

Method: Analytical stability analysis of two self-similar solutions describing weak shock propagation in failed supernovae; investigation of Mach number growth as a power law in time; identification of a critical mass loss threshold above which shocks asymptotically approach the strong limit.

Result: Larger Mach number self-similar solutions are unstable, with Mach number growing as ∝ t^α (α ≲ 0.1); smaller Mach number solutions are stable. Above a critical relative mass loss (readily achievable in core-collapse SNe), the shock asymptotically strengthens toward the strong-shock limit.

Conclusion: The key parameters governing shock strength and ejecta mass are the fractional mass lost to neutrinos relative to the mass enclosed by the shock and the stellar density gradient at shock formation. This explains why red supergiants produce more luminous transients compared to more compact progenitors.

Abstract: Some high-mass stars likely end their lives in underluminous implosions that leave behind a black hole, known as failed supernovae (FSNe). However, neutrinos radiated during proto-neutron star formation generate a weak (Mach $\gtrsim 1$) shockwave in the outer layers of the star, which produces a unique transient as it breaks out of the dying star and signals its imminent disappearance. It was recently shown that there are two self-similar solutions that describe the propagation of this weak shockwave, and these solutions simultaneously contain outward-moving ejecta and fallback accretion onto the black hole. Here we show that the larger Mach number solutions are unstable, such that the Mach number of the shock grows with time $t$ and deviates from the self-similar prediction as $\propto t^α$, with $α\lesssim 0.1$, whereas the smaller Mach number solutions are stable. We also show that, above a critical mass loss that is readily achievable in core-collapse supernovae, the shock asymptotically strengthens and approaches the strong limit. Our results imply that it is the mass lost to neutrinos \textit{relative} to the mass enclosed by the shockwave, as well as the stellar density gradient where the shock forms, that primarily dictate its strength and the amount of material it ejects. These criteria explain why red supergiants, which have relative mass losses well in excess of the critical value at the time of shock formation, more readily eject material and create more luminous explosions compared to more compact progenitors.

Abstract: The degree of black-hole spin-orbit misalignment ("tilts") in the astrophysical population could be a powerful diagnostic to distinguish between binary formation in isolation, in dynamical environments, or in hierarchical triples.
